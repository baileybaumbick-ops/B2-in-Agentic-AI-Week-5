# Class 5 - Competitive Advantage

## Definition
Competitive advantage is the ability to be consistently more profitable than competitors in the same market or industry. Earlier classes covered value creation and capture and which industries allow long-run profit (Five Forces); this class asks how some firms in an industry create and capture value better than others.

Competitive advantage makes a firm irreplaceable, but not every irreplaceable firm has a competitive advantage. DRAM makers or shipping lines are irreplaceable when capacity is tight, yet they are all roughly the same. A firm with a competitive advantage is irreplaceable in a way that differs from its rivals, so you have to compare it to other firms doing something else and think about what entrants will do.

## Value proposition
Firms in the same industry create value in different ways: Toyota (high-volume, reliable, practical cars) versus Porsche (low-volume, performance, distinctive styling); Uniqlo (quality basics with little style variety) versus Zara (fast-changing trend items); Apple (integrated hardware and software, ease of use, design) versus Microsoft (software with broad compatibility across third-party hardware). A value proposition is a short, precise statement of how the firm intends to create value, its plan for opening the gap between benefit B and cost C.

## The AAA framework
Assets, Activities, Advantage: assets enable activities, and activities create competitive advantage when they fit the context.
- Assets are what the firm has: resources (equipment, location, brand, IP, people, knowledge) and capabilities (developing products, managing customers, procurement, inventory control, motivating staff). Today's assets come from past choices.
- Activities are what the firm chooses to do with its assets. Strategy lives in the activities; the value proposition summarizes them.
- Advantage results when the activities suit the context.

Roche example: Roche focused on low-volume conditions (cancer, AIDS, transplantation) and biotech drugs rather than blockbuster pills. Each dose costs hundreds or thousands of dollars, it needs only a small sales force (about 1,600 marketers versus 9,000 at Glaxo and 11,000 at Pfizer) because it sells to specialists, and biotech drugs are harder to copy. The assets it needs are scientific "finders" who can spot promising biotech partners and expertise in clinical trials. Its advantage is in commercializing science that others discover.

Netflix and advertising: in 2020 Reed Hastings said Netflix would not sell ads, because Google, Amazon and Facebook had far better data assets, so Netflix would be strategically disadvantaged. Netflix launched an ad tier in 2022 and in 2024 announced its own ad-tech platform once it had invested in the needed assets.

## Three sources of competitive advantage
The sources mirror the ways to create value: benefit-based, cost-based, and niche (quantity)-based.

Cost-based: Chevron used sensors and machine learning for predictive maintenance on about 5,000 heat exchangers, saving millions. Porter argues operational excellence alone is not a competitive advantage, but it could be one if Chevron has more or better data or gets ahead and stays ahead. AMD under Lisa Su bet on PC and server CPUs and graphics when conventional wisdom favored mobile, paid $325 million to exit its contract with GlobalFoundries so TSMC could make its chips, and invested in graphics despite NVIDIA.

Benefit-based: Johnson & Johnson's brand certified quality for commodity products like children's Tylenol; the premium over the store brand was about 50% in 2019 ($7.49 versus $4.99). Supporting that brand required top-quality manufacturing and transparency. Around 2010, weak quality control, cost cutting and a "phantom recall" damaged the brand and pushed shoppers to generics.

Niche-based: find a subset of customers where price rivalry is reduced, protected by a barrier to entry. Horizontal differentiation means customers disagree about the ideal product (music, fashion, cars). Vertical differentiation means customers agree which product is better but differ in how much they will pay for quality. Kam Pek Casino in Macau targets low rollers; since Macau dealers are expensive (1.8% unemployment, local-hire rules), it uses live dealers on video so 8 dealers serve 900 gamblers instead of 100 dealers, keeping labor cost 30% below other casinos. Dell sold direct to knowledgeable corporate buyers, skipping reseller markups; IBM tried to copy it but angered its resellers and its manufacturing was built for standard products.

Casper shows the limit: bed-in-a-box grew fast, but dozens of copycats entered because there was no entry barrier, and Casper never became profitable. Bombas used buy-one-give-one; TOMS lost its edge when rivals copied the model, and Warby Parker scaled its donations back because giving away identical products is costly.

## Case - Enterprise Rent-A-Car
Enterprise was consistently the most profitable US rental car company. Its choices reinforce each other:
- Locations: it served people who are not traveling and skipped the crowded airport market, opening many small, convenient, low-cost offices; about 90% of the US population lives within 15 minutes of one.
- Referrals: close relationships with body shops and insurers, integrated through its ARMS/ECARS systems, with staff placed at insurers. Insurers pay for the rental, so Enterprise competes on service rather than price.
- Organization: hires sociable college graduates, pays for performance tied to branch profit, promotes from within; runs its own reservation and inventory systems and sells used cars through about 30 lots.

Locations justify referrals, referrals justify locations, and the organization sustains both, which creates a chicken-and-egg problem for Avis or Hertz trying to copy it. One risk is that promote-from-within needs constant growth.
