# Class 6 - Entry and Positioning

## Facts about entry and exit
Entry and exit are pervasive; entrants and exiting firms tend to be smaller than established firms; most entrants do not survive 10 years, but those that survive grow quickly; and entry and exit rates vary by industry. Implications: plan for an unknown future competitor, expect most new ventures to fail, and know how important entry is in your own industry. Entry is broader than new firms: it includes new markets (M-Pesa entering India, Amazon entering grocery) and new products (Ozempic, Vanilla Coke, the microprocessor).

## Counter-positioning
An entrant has to choose a position: a price and a bundle of features. When you enter you take customers from incumbents, and incumbents may react. Counter-positioning means choosing a position the incumbent does not want to fight, because fighting would cost it too much with its existing customers.

## Water bottle exercise
Two products: a high-quality insulated bottle (marginal cost $10, every customer values it at $20) and a low-quality plastic bottle (marginal cost $5). There are 10 customers: 2 value the plastic bottle at $15 and 8 value it at $10. A monopolist would sell the insulated bottle at $20 to all 10 for a profit of $100.

Now an incumbent sells the insulated bottle at $20 and you enter:
- Copy the insulated bottle at $19: the incumbent undercuts to $18.99, which leads toward a price war. Direct attack invites retaliation.
- Plastic bottle at $14 (only $1 of surplus to the 2 high-value customers): retaliating by cutting to $18.99 earns the incumbent $90 versus $80 from accommodating, so it retaliates.
- Plastic bottle at $11 (gives those 2 customers $4 of surplus): winning them back needs a price of $15.99, which earns $60 versus $80 from accommodating, so the incumbent accommodates and you earn $12.
- Below $10 you would attract everyone, so retaliation is certain.

Intuition: to win back a few customers the incumbent must lower its price to all of them. Target a small segment and price low enough that fighting is not worth it. If the targeted segment is larger, you must price lower; if it is smaller, you can price higher. Counter-positioning works better when the incumbent would find it hard to make your product well.

## Entry examples
- Southwest positioned against driving, using secondary airports, point-to-point routes and one aircraft type. Rivals mostly accommodated by retreating to long-haul and business routes.
- Warby Parker sold prescription glasses at $95 direct to consumers, bypassing Luxottica. Luxottica could not match the price without cannibalizing its own stores and licensing business, so it accommodated.
- Google Docs (2006) entered as a low-end alternative; Microsoft responded with Office 365 in 2011 and sped it up from 2014.
- Perplexity attacked Google Search directly with an answer engine. Google retaliated with AI Overviews from 2024, even though they reduce ad clicks.
- Mounjaro (Eli Lilly) arrived five years after Ozempic (Novo Nordisk) and gained share on better outcomes.
- SpaceX entered space launch after Boeing and Lockheed Martin formed the United Launch Alliance in 2006; Falcon 9 was certified for national security launches in 2015.

## Case - Tesla
The Model S was Tesla's first real production car (about 10,000 a year) and the first entry by a new US carmaker since WWII. In H1 2013 regulatory ZEV credits were 12% of Tesla's revenue; without them its operating margin was -16%, against BMW's +11% in 2012. The lecture showed that learning-curve and scale effects could make the numbers much better than they looked for a start-up.

Barriers in autos were high: brand and advertising (Ford spends about $4 billion a year), plants costing billions with MES of 100,000-250,000 cars, about 1,000 suppliers per model, large dealer networks (Toyota about 1,600 US dealers; Ford and GM about 4,000), and years of design capability. Tesla got around them: the Roadster built its brand; it bought the Fremont factory for $42 million; EVs are simpler (far fewer engine parts, no transmission, no cooling system), so fewer suppliers and capabilities were needed; it partnered with Lotus and Panasonic early; and it sold directly instead of through dealers.

Musk's 2006 "master plan" was to start at the high end, where customers pay a premium, and move down-market with each model. Takeaways: exploit a wave of change and incumbents' inertia; sequence positions and capabilities; start at the high end where nobody has scale. Tesla's durable advantage may lie in software, batteries and charging rather than car making. In 2025 GM sold about 170,000 EVs versus Tesla's 1.64 million. Later entrants: Rivian (adventure lifestyle plus a 100,000-van order from Amazon) and Lucid (luxury, above Tesla, funded by a Saudi fund).
