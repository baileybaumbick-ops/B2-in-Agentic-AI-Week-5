# Class 4 - Industry Analysis

## Five Forces as a starting point
Porter's Five Forces asks how much value an industry can create and how much of it firms can keep as profit. It is a checklist, not an answer: it makes sure you have covered the parts of the context that matter, but it does not tell you what to do. The five forces are substitutes, buyer power, supplier power, entry, and rivalry. Later classes on competitive advantage ask why some firms in an industry earn more than others.

## Substitutes
A substitute meets the same need as your product. Its effect on profit is similar to an entrant with a different product. Which products count as substitutes depends on context.

## Buyer power and supplier power
Buyers can push prices down when they are few and large, when there are many substitutes for your product, and when they could easily bypass you or make the product themselves. Supplier power is buyer power turned around: suppliers extract high prices when they face little competition, have few substitutes, and when you cannot easily make the input yourself.

Beef example: ranchers used to get about 50 cents of each retail beef dollar; in 2021 they got 37 cents, even with record retail prices. The four largest meatpackers (Tyson, Cargill, National Beef and JBS) grew from 36% to 85% market share since the 1980s, cut capacity during the pandemic, and buy through private contracts with feedlots, which reduces price transparency.

## Entry and barriers to entry
Profitable industries attract entrants and imitators, which lowers prices and splits profit, so the entry force looks at potential rivals, not just current ones. Barriers to entry make a market hard to enter or less profitable for entrants than incumbents. Barriers include economies of scale relative to market size, scarce resources (a good location, access to inputs or distribution), learning curves, network effects, and intellectual property.

Minimum efficient scale (MES) is the smallest output that reaches minimum unit cost. The ratio of MES to industry demand gives the maximum number of efficient firms: cement has few plants, dry cleaning has many.

A direct network effect means each user values the product more as more people use it (languages, phones, social media). An indirect or platform network effect means one type of user benefits from more users of another type (buyers and sellers).

IP matters a lot in pharmaceuticals: once a drug is on the market it can be reverse engineered, so billions in R&D need enforceable patents.

## Rivalry
Rivalry is how intensely current rivals compete. Price rivalry hurts profit most; rivalry in quality or brand can be offset by higher prices. Price rivalry is fierce when small price cuts win many sales: homogeneous products, low search costs, low switching costs, low brand loyalty, many sellers, frequent purchases, high fixed costs, and excess capacity.

## Example - EV charging
Rivalry: fueling has always been low margin (gas stations earn most from their convenience stores), and electricity is hard to differentiate except by location and charging speed. Entry and substitutes: a chicken-and-egg problem between cars and chargers, and home charging, which is about 80% of EV charging. Buyer power: switching between networks is easy (Tesla was the exception), and density cuts both ways. Supplier power: landowners with good sites, slow and costly grid connections, and expensive equipment. Business models: sell equipment (ChargePoint), own chargers and charge per use (EVgo), advertising screens (Volta), or a restaurant model.

By December 2023 shares had fallen about 70% for ChargePoint, 67% for Blink and 20% for EVgo. By October 2025 the network had matured: fast-charging ports rose more than 80% in two years to over 60,300, and failed charging visits fell to 14%, just as the $7,500 federal EV tax credit expired.

## Case - Cola Wars
Carbonated soft drinks are about 27% of US beverage consumption and $48 billion in US sales. Coke and Pepsi hold about 70% of the market, and concentrate producers earn gross profit around 83% of net sales, versus 25-35% for an average US firm.

Five Forces: substitutes are everywhere (water, coffee, juice, beer), but Coke kept its product "within arm's reach of desire." Buyer power ranges from low to high; profitability for concentrate producers ranks vending > convenience stores > grocery > fountain. Supplier power is low: concentrate costs about $0.11 per case and inputs are commodities. Rivalry: the two colas are nearly identical, so a price war would be ruinous; instead they compete on advertising and marketing (about 39% of net sales versus about 10% for an average firm) and on new flavors, moving competition from price to dimensions that grow the market. They fight harder abroad, where Pepsi is often a small entrant. Entry barriers: scale in distribution and marketing, established channels, limited shelf space, and brand; a concentrate plant costs only a few million dollars, so plant cost itself is not the barrier.

## Takeaways
The cola industry is profitable because buyers and suppliers are weak and entry barriers are high, and Coke and Pepsi shaped competition by creating perceived differentiation. The Five Forces focus on splitting a fixed pie and leave out joint value creation, such as legally growing the market together. If the forces say an industry is attractive, ask why more firms have not entered: either entry is hard or the industry has changed.
