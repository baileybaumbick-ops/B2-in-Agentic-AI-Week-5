# Class 3 - Added Value and Irreplaceability

## Irreplaceability drives value capture
How much value a firm captures depends on the price it can charge. If nobody else can do what you do, customers have no easy alternative and you can price well above cost. If many firms can do it, customers choose on price and competition pushes price toward cost. Irreplaceability is not the only reason firms fail to capture value, but it is the most common one.

## Defining added value
Added value = total value created with you in the market minus total value created without you. Put differently: how much would everyone else together pay to keep you in? A player should not expect to get more than its added value, because the others would be better off letting it walk away and splitting what remains.

Capacity shows how added value moves. Before the AI boom, DRAM makers were easy to replace (a buyer who skipped Samsung bought Micron), so prices were low and SK Hynix lost money in 2023. With AI demand, capacity is tight: OpenAI's projected need of up to 900,000 DRAM wafers a month is more than double the industry's HBM capacity, so each supplier is hard to replace and profits jumped. Red Sea attacks in 2025 forced rerouting around the Cape of Good Hope, straining shipping capacity and raising profits for carriers like Maersk.

## Joint venture exercise
Three firms can form partnerships with these profits: all three together $100M; A and B $80M; A and C $70M; B and C $40M; any firm alone $15M.

The three-firm venture creates the most value. Added values: A = 100 - 40 = $60M; B = 100 - 70 = $30M; C = 100 - 80 = $20M.

Upper bound: a firm should get at most its added value. If A took more than $60M, B and C would get less than the $40M they can earn alone and would leave.

Lower bound: a firm should get at least its outside option (BATNA), here $15M, and more when the others have low added value. A should get no less than 100 - (30 + 20) = $50M, so A's predicted share is between $50M and $60M.

Key principles: raise your own added value, raise your outside option, and lower other players' added value to improve your negotiating position.

## Examples of shifting added value
Apple and OLED screens: before 2018 Samsung was the only firm able to make OLED screens at iPhone X size in enough volume; the display was $97 of the phone's $376 production cost. Apple got LG to start making OLED phone screens, and Samsung lost its irreplaceability.

Australia's News Media Bargaining Code (2021) tried to make Google and Facebook pay news outlets. Google threatened to pull search and Facebook briefly blocked news, but both eventually paid, around $1 billion in total. When the three-year deals expired, Meta said it would not renew and would remove news rather than be designated under the code.

## Case - Intel
In DRAM, Intel's 1972 strategy was innovation and product design, being first to market, pricing high and moving on. That fit a new market where innovation mattered more than scale. By 1979 the market grew fast, product cycles shrank, scale mattered more, and Japanese producers with better manufacturing entered. Intel's added value in DRAM collapsed, so it moved to a market that fit its strengths: microprocessors. Lessons from DRAM: protect IP, commodities are hard to profit from, and manufacturing yield matters.

In the early PC years IBM was the most irreplaceable player thanks to its mainframe reputation ("nobody ever got fired for buying IBM"), which let it set the architecture standard. IBM's open model brought many partners and growth but lower margins and less control; Apple's closed model kept margin and control but grew slower.

By the 1990s the power had shifted to Intel and Microsoft ("Wintel"). The reason was network effects: apps are written for an OS, the OS talks to the processor, users go where the apps are, and developers go where the users are. Unlike a hard drive, you cannot swap out the OS or processor without breaking the apps. Watershed events: Compaq reverse-engineered the BIOS, so compatibility required Intel rather than IBM, and IBM delayed the 386 while it pushed its proprietary PS/2 and OS/2, so the market moved on without it. Intel's customers said they would need more than a 2x performance gap before switching to RISC.

Intel captured its added value through Intel Inside (co-op ad money that PC makers could not refuse, a prisoner's dilemma that commoditized them), a systems business that showed PC makers they were replaceable, rationing new chips by past sales to discourage switching to AMD, and IP lawsuits against AMD.

In 2018 Intel had little added value in modem chips (Qualcomm owned the IP and was faster), but Apple used Intel modems in half its phones during a royalty dispute, which shifted bargaining power from Qualcomm to Apple.

## Takeaways
Being irreplaceable is key to capturing value, and added value measures irreplaceability. Intel's added value vanished in DRAM but persisted in microprocessors because network effects locked in developers and users. The lecture closed by asking what NVIDIA is trying to do with CUDA.
