# Class 1 - Introduction to Strategy

## Why strategy is hard
Profit is revenue minus cost, so "raise revenue, cut cost" sounds easy. The lecture argued that good strategy is hard for three reasons: decisions can have counterintuitive effects that change outcomes a lot, you have to predict how other players will respond to your choices, and the world is noisy, so you must figure out which few factors actually matter. The two tools for making progress are understanding the context and dissecting the trade-offs.

A warm-up example: a heavy-machinery CEO is offered technology that makes plants easy to repurpose or sell. Flexibility sounds good, but if a potential entrant is watching, being able to walk away easily can weaken your threat to fight entry. This previews the entry-deterrence ideas later in the course.

## Context matters
The same move can be great for one firm and a disaster for another. Context includes who the buyers are, the cost structure, competitors and their interests, legal and regulatory limits, internal incentives, and assets such as brand, location, technology and IP.

Amazon buying Whole Foods (2017) made sense for Amazon but would not for FedEx or Uber: it increased Prime stickiness and gave Amazon shopper data, gave it roughly 500 stores to use as last-mile delivery hubs for Amazon Fresh, and defended against Walmart in physical retail.

M-Pesa succeeded in Kenya because of urban-to-rural remittances, more than 80,000 cash-out agents, light regulation (it was treated as a telecom product, not banking), and Safaricom being the dominant carrier. India looked promising (over 600 million unbanked people, about 900 million mobile subscribers) but differed: mobile money is regulated as banking there, and Vodafone was the number-two carrier, not number one.

## Trade-offs and opportunity cost
Firms do not control revenue or cost directly; they control operating decisions that move both, usually in opposite directions (higher quality raises willingness to pay but also cost; more volume usually needs lower prices). Examples of trade-offs: Netflix's password-sharing crackdown, Apple's App Tracking Transparency, Disney launching streaming and cannibalizing cable, Starbucks mobile ordering hurting the in-store experience, and Google adding AI answers that may reduce ad clicks.

Opportunity cost is the value of the best alternative you give up. Disney gave up more than $300 million a year in licensing fees when it pulled content from Netflix for Disney+.

## Defining strategy
The lecture quoted Porter (1996): a company can outperform rivals only if it can establish a difference it can preserve. In innovative industries these differences close quickly, so innovation must be continual. Working definition: strategy is a plan of action to achieve the firm's goal (profit) that establishes a persistent difference over rivals, by dissecting context and trade-offs to understand how others will respond and which features of the environment matter.

Bad strategy is different from bad luck: Porsche decided in 2019 to make the next Macan electric-only, then in 2025 added combustion and hybrid versions back, saying it would have made the same call on the data available at the time.

## The economic approach
Economics frames decisions by identifying decision makers, goals, choices and outputs, then moving from describe to predict to prescribe. Decision makers include rivals, suppliers, regulators and start-ups, not just your own executives. Firms are assumed to maximize profit. Use economic costs rather than accounting costs: ignore sunk costs and include opportunity costs.

Equilibrium is the "rest point" of a system: environmental changes create threats and opportunities, which drive entry and exit, and competition pushes the market back toward equilibrium.

## Case - Moneyball
The Oakland A's had a small payroll compared with rich teams. Scouts valued players partly on looks (the "Good Face"). The A's found that on-base percentage predicted wins better than slugging, yet sluggers were paid far more, which was an arbitrage opportunity for cheap wins. Around 2000, wins from OBP were much cheaper than wins from slugging; by about 2004-2005 the market price and the true value of OBP had converged. Lessons: the A's found a way to lower the cost of wins, but markets move toward equilibrium, so the advantage did not last.
