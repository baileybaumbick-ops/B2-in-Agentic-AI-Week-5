# Class 2 - Value Creation and Capture

## Profit maximization as the goal
Why assume firms maximize profit? A firm that ignores profit goes bankrupt, cannot fund the investments it needs, and gets its assets redeployed by the capital market (for example through a takeover). Long-run profit maximization is not quarterly earnings management, short-run exploitation of employees, suppliers or customers, or unethical shortcuts.

Social impact: for firms whose customers value it, social impact is part of the value proposition, so long-run profit and social impact line up. For firms whose customers do not value it, competition makes costly social goals hard to sustain, and regulation is needed to level the field. Large institutional shareholders can push managers either because they have pro-social preferences or because they believe it raises long-run profits. The course tools also apply to non-profits: how to best reach a social objective with limited resources.

"Maximize profit" is a goal, not a strategy, in the same way "win" is a coach's goal. Strategy is the set of decisions (inputs, processes, products, customers, prices) that lead to long-run profit.

## The profit function
Profit = (price - cost per unit) x quantity. A strategy raises profit only by raising price, lowering unit cost, or raising quantity. The levers interact: H&H Bail Bonds tried to raise quantity by loosening standards, but that also raised costs.

## Value created and value captured
Value created is the gap between the customer's benefit (willingness to pay, B) and the cost (C). The price P splits it: B - P is consumer surplus and P - C is the firm's profit. A firm must create value before it can capture it.

To create more value a firm can increase benefits, decrease costs, or increase quantity (new markets, new customers, more usage). Examples: Amazon and IKEA lower cost; Uber and Spotify raise benefit; pharmaceutical firms find new patient groups.

Jay-Z moved his music exclusively to Tidal in 2017 (about 3 million paying users) and returned to Spotify (about 113 million paying users) in 2019. Owning the whole pie mattered less than the pie being much smaller: a smaller slice of a bigger pie can be more.

Allbirds used sustainable materials, which raised willingness to pay only for sustainability-minded buyers while raising cost for all, so other buyers went elsewhere; it later closed its stores to focus on e-commerce.

## Why firms fail to capture value
Creating value does not guarantee profit. Firms fail to capture value if they do not really create value, if the party that benefits is not the party that pays, or if competition drives prices down. They capture more by being irreplaceable and by innovating on pricing or revenue models.

Netflix's 2023 password-sharing crackdown was a bet that shared users would buy their own accounts rather than quit (costs up a little, benefit down a little, quantity up a lot). Game studios adding NFTs in 2022 angered players, a drop in benefit, echoing Blizzard's Diablo III auction house in 2013.

## Astroscale and public goods
Space debris in low earth orbit (tens of millions of pieces moving around 17,000 mph) causes collisions, and each collision creates more debris. Cleaning it up is hard for practical reasons (small, fast, hard to see), legal reasons (the launching country owns each object) and economic reasons: debris is a negative externality and cleanup is a public good, non-rival and non-excludable. Astroscale's ELSA satellite uses an adhesive plate to catch debris and steer it into the atmosphere. Its revenue plan: sell mapping data, contract with satellite operators to remove their own dead satellites (a private good), and eventually be paid for general cleanup by a party like NASA or a consortium.

## Case - CF Industries
CF sells nitrogen fertilizer, a commodity, yet was very profitable. Fertilizer's value depends on being in the right place in a window of a few weeks. CF created value by having ammonia where and when farmers needed it: 29 storage terminals across the Midwest (it rents storage at about $150 per ton), multiple transport modes including about 5,000 owned or leased rail cars, and demand forecasting. It lowered cost thanks to cheap natural gas (70-80% of ammonia production cost), running expensive plants (about $1 billion each) with minimal downtime, and preventive maintenance.

CF captured value through its terminals: ammonia is hazardous and needs special storage, new terminals are heavily restricted by regulation (an entry barrier), and storage lets CF sell to the higher-priced agricultural market rather than the industrial market, which paid $150-200 less per ton. That is why it acquired Terra. It also captured value through better pricing information and pricing innovation (forward sales hedged with natural gas swaps, and "summer fill" programs that price discriminate between farmers with and without storage). Its $3.8 billion expansion led Agrium, Yara and Mosaic to cancel their own plans, a form of entry deterrence.
